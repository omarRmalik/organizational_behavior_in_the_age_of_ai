# Preface

## Organizational Behavior at a Turning Point

Organizational behavior grew out of a simple observation: organizations are made of people, and understanding people is the key to managing organizations well.

Over the past century, OB research has given managers powerful tools. We know a great deal about how personality relates to job performance, how job design shapes motivation, how leaders build high-quality relationships, how groups share (and fail to share) information, how diversity can strengthen or strain teams, and how organizations learn.

Artificial intelligence does not make this knowledge obsolete.

It changes the situations to which the knowledge must be applied.

A growing share of employees now work with systems that search, summarize, predict, recommend, evaluate, and sometimes decide. The *Journal of Organizational Behavior* recently devoted a special issue to the question of whether "our future colleague" will even be human (Ulfert et al., 2026). The *Journal of Management Studies* and *Personnel Psychology* have published extensive work on AI in hiring, performance evaluation, professional work, and employee well-being.

This course brings that research into the undergraduate classroom.

# Why Organizational Behavior Needs Updating

Classic OB theories rest on assumptions that were reasonable when they were developed:

| Classic Assumption | What AI Changes |
|---|---|
| Knowledge is created by people through experience and conversation | AI generates, organizes, and recombines knowledge |
| Decisions are made by people, individually or in groups | AI recommends, ranks, and sometimes makes decisions |
| Trust is built between people | Employees must calibrate trust in systems they cannot fully inspect |
| Motivation depends on how managers design jobs | Algorithms increasingly assign, monitor, and evaluate work |
| Leaders influence followers through relationships | Leaders collaborate with AI and share influence with it |
| Groups fail when members do not share information | AI can pool information — and create new asymmetries |
| Fairness depends on managers' judgments | Fairness depends on data, model design, and how people use outputs |
| Expertise gives professionals authority | AI challenges who holds expertise and authority |

Each week of the course examines one row of this table in depth.

# The Central Question

> **How does organizational behavior change when humans and AI jointly develop knowledge and make decisions?**

Students return to this question in every class discussion, every reading reflection, and every project milestone.

---

# Educational Philosophy

## 1. The Foundations Still Matter

Students cannot evaluate how AI changes OB without first understanding OB. The textbook and the classic articles provide that foundation.

## 2. Assumptions Are Where Theories Break

Every theory rests on assumptions. Students learn to find the assumptions about *who knows* and *who decides*, because that is where AI puts the most pressure on existing theory.

## 3. Evidence Beats Opinion

Claims about AI at work are everywhere. This course relies on peer-reviewed research. Students learn to distinguish what studies show from what commentators predict.

## 4. Writing Deepens Understanding

Students sharpen their thinking by writing about it. Weekly reflections make abstract concepts — trust calibration, knowledge co-creation, hidden profiles, algorithmic fairness — concrete by asking students to apply them and propose how theory should change.

## 5. People Come First

Effective, fair, and humane work is the goal. AI is valuable when it helps people and organizations achieve that goal.

---

# Pedagogical Model

The course follows a three-part weekly cycle.

## Learn

Students read the textbook chapter and the classic article, and review the core concepts in class.

## Question

Students read the contemporary AI article and identify which assumptions of the classic theory it supports, challenges, or changes.

## Apply

Students discuss a Harvard Business School case, write a reflection, and apply the week's ideas to their team's Human–AI Work Study.

---

# Signature Learning Experiences

## Weekly Reading Reflections and OB Theory Updates

Nine short reflections (Part VI), one each week from Week 2 to Week 10. Each reflection ends with a short theory update in response to the prompt:

> *"If this OB theory were rewritten for workplaces where people and AI build knowledge and make decisions together, I would change…"*

## The Human–AI Work Study

A team project (Part VII) in which students analyze how a real organization's people and AI systems know and decide together, and recommend improvements.

## Case Discussions

Harvard Business School cases, including the deployment of Microsoft Copilot, Dessa, Google's Project Oxygen, Governing OpenAI, Trouble at Tessei, Microsoft's culture transformation, and JPMorganChase's leadership in the age of GenAI, connect research to real managerial choices.

---

# Expected Student Mindset

Students succeed in this course when they:

- come to class prepared to discuss the readings;
- treat AI as a partner to question, not an authority to obey;
- notice their own reactions to AI — trust, doubt, relief, frustration — as data about OB;
- support claims with evidence;
- and treat classmates as collaborators in building knowledge.

# What Makes This Course Different

| Traditional OB Course | This Course |
|---|---|
| Teaches established theories | Teaches established theories and tests them against AI-era evidence |
| Treats AI as a side topic, if at all | Treats human–AI collaboration as the context for every topic |
| Relies mostly on the textbook | Pairs the textbook with classic and current research articles |
| Assesses mostly through exams | Assesses through reflections, a team project, and exams |
| Discusses technology in the abstract | Examines peer-reviewed evidence on how people work with AI |

The course prepares students not only to *know* organizational behavior, but to *update* it.
