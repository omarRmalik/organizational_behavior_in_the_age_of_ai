# Overview

The course meets twice a week for ten weeks, followed by a finals week. Each week follows the same rhythm:

1. Textbook chapter — core concepts
2. Classic OB article — the foundational theory
3. Contemporary AI article — what changes
4. Human–AI Learning Lab — experience the change
5. Case discussion (most weeks) — apply it to a real organization
6. Reflection and OB Theory Update — make sense of it
7. Human–AI Work Study — apply it to the team's organization

**Textbook:** Hitt, Miller, Colella, and Triana, *Organizational Behavior* (5th ed.). Wiley. ISBN 978-1-119-39173-9.

Chapter numbers for Chapters 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, and 10 follow the 5th edition as used in prior offerings. Chapters marked **[confirm]** should be checked against the edition adopted for the term.

# Module Details

## Week 1 — OB When Humans and AI Think Together

Introduces OB, its three levels of analysis, and the central question of the course. Simon's bounded rationality explains why people have always needed help to know and decide; Bankins et al. map what AI is already changing at each level. Students meet the JKD Framework and complete Lab 1.

**Guiding question:** What does classic OB assume about who knows and who decides?

## Week 2 — Personality, Attitudes, and Trust in AI

Covers personality, attitudes, and emotions. Mayer, Davis, and Schoorman's model of trust (ability, benevolence, integrity) is tested against Vuori et al.'s finding that emotional and cognitive trust in AI can diverge — producing "uncomfortable" and "blind" trust. The Copilot case examines employee attitudes during an AI rollout.

**Supplementary:** Barrick & Mount (1991); Erengin et al. (2025).

**Guiding question:** Can the model of interpersonal trust explain trust in AI?

## Week 3 — Learning and Perception with Algorithms

Covers learning, perception, and attribution. Nonaka explains how knowledge is created through the interplay of tacit and explicit knowledge. Anthony shows how knowledge workers sometimes "black box" analytical tools — relying on outputs they no longer understand.

**Supplementary:** Kim et al. (2025); Jia et al. (2024).

**Guiding question:** When does working with AI help people learn, and when does it stop them from learning?

## Week 4 — Motivation and Work Design

Covers motivation theories and job design. Hackman and Oldham's job characteristics model is applied by Schulz et al. to explain why AI adoption can both enrich and impoverish jobs. The Tessei case examines motivation and meaning in routine work.

**Supplementary:** Rahman (2021); Bellesia et al. (2024); Klonek & Parker (2025); Kellogg et al. (2020).

**Guiding question:** Does AI give workers more autonomy and feedback — or less?

## Week 5 — Leadership with AI (Midterm)

Covers leadership theories. Graen and Uhl-Bien's leader–member exchange theory is set against Liu et al.'s study of how leaders' collaboration with AI affects team performance. Project Oxygen examines what makes managers effective, using people analytics. The midterm covers Weeks 1–4.

**Supplementary:** He et al. (2025).

**Guiding question:** If a leader works closely with AI, what happens to the leader's relationships with followers?

## Week 6 — Group Decision Making

Covers individual and group decision making. Stasser and Titus show that groups tend to discuss information everyone already has and miss unshared information. Zercher et al. test whether a generative AI team member reduces or creates such asymmetries. The JPMorganChase case examines leadership decisions about GenAI at scale.

**Supplementary:** Lee et al. (2025); Shrestha et al. (2019).

**Guiding question:** Does AI make group decisions better, worse, or just different?

## Week 7 — Teams, Trust, and Psychological Safety

Covers teams and team processes. Edmondson's psychological safety is extended to human–AI teams: can team members speak up about an AI teammate's error? Erengin et al. show that human teammates shape trust in AI teammates. The Governing OpenAI case examines decision authority and accountability at the top of an AI organization.

**Supplementary:** Ulfert et al. (2026); Dellermann et al. (2019).

**Guiding question:** Who is responsible when a human–AI team gets it wrong?

## Week 8 — Diversity, Inclusion, and Algorithmic Fairness

Covers organizational diversity. Ely and Thomas show that how an organization frames diversity shapes its effects. van den Broek et al.'s ethnography of AI in hiring shows how fairness is negotiated among HR staff, managers, and developers. The Dessa case examines building an inclusive AI company.

**Supplementary:** Zhang et al. (2023); Triana & Upadhyay (2026).

**Guiding question:** Can an algorithm be fair if the organization using it is not?

## Week 9 — Culture, Expertise, and Organizational Learning

Covers culture and organizational learning. March's exploration–exploitation balance frames how AI can accelerate learning or entrench routines. Faulconbridge et al. show how professionals redraw the boundaries of their expertise around AI. The Microsoft case examines a shift from a "know-it-all" to a "learn-it-all" culture.

**Supplementary:** Scarbrough et al. (2024); Mayer, van den Broek & Karačić (2025); Makarius et al. (2020).

**Guiding question:** What kind of culture helps people and AI learn together?

## Week 10 — Toward an OB of Human–AI Joint Agency

Synthesis. Murray, Rhymer, and Sirmon's concept of conjoined agency and Stelmaszak et al.'s view of AI as a capability arising from human–algorithm relations help students integrate the course. Teams present their Human–AI Work Studies.

**Guiding question:** What would organizational behavior look like if it were written today?

## Week 11 — Finals Week

Final Human–AI Work Study report due. Final examination during the scheduled exam period.

# Developmental Learning Progression

## Week 1: Foundations

Students learn what OB studies and why human–AI collaboration unsettles its assumptions.

## Weeks 2–4: The Individual

Students examine how a single person trusts, learns from, and is motivated (or demotivated) by working with AI.

## Weeks 5–7: The Group

Students examine how leaders, groups, and teams share knowledge and decisions with AI.

## Weeks 8–9: The Organization

Students examine fairness, culture, expertise, and learning across the whole organization.

## Weeks 10–11: Integration

Students bring the levels together in their own theory updates and team project.

---

# Recurring Learning Activities

## Scholarly Dialogue

Classic and contemporary readings are discussed side by side each week.

## Human–AI Learning Lab

A 30–40 minute in-class lab in which students complete an OB task with an AI tool (Part VI).

## Reflection and OB Theory Update

A weekly written reflection on the lab and the readings, ending with a proposed theory update.

## Harvard Business School Case Discussion

Case discussions on seven weeks connect research to managerial decisions. Participation is graded on case days.

## Human–AI Work Study

Milestones in Weeks 3, 6, and 8 build toward the draft report (Week 9), presentation (Week 10), and final report (Week 11).

---

# The Thread That Connects Every Week

Every module asks the same question at a different level and on a different topic:

> **How does organizational behavior change when humans and AI jointly develop knowledge and make decisions?**

By Week 10, students should be able to answer it for themselves — with evidence.
