# Part VI
# Human–AI Learning Labs

> *"You cannot fully understand working with AI by reading about it. You have to notice what happens to your own thinking when you do it."*

---

# Introduction

The Human–AI Learning Labs turn the central question of the course into direct experience.

Each week, students complete a short organizational behavior task together with an AI tool. Then they examine the collaboration itself: What did the AI contribute? What did I contribute? Did my knowledge or my decision change — and was the change an improvement?

The labs are not exercises in using AI efficiently. They are small experiments in organizational behavior, with the student as both participant and observer.

---

# Learning Philosophy

The labs are built on four ideas:

1. **Experience before theory.** Students feel trust, over-reliance, or resistance before they read about it.
2. **Compare with and without AI.** Most labs ask students to work alone first, then with AI, so the effect of the AI is visible.
3. **Verify, don't obey.** Students check AI outputs against the textbook, the readings, and each other.
4. **Reflect on yourself.** A student's own reactions to AI are evidence about how people behave with AI.

# Standard Lab Structure

Each lab follows five steps and takes 30–40 minutes of class time.

## Step 1 — Solo Attempt

Students begin the task alone, without AI, and record their initial answer or decision.

## Step 2 — Human–AI Collaboration

Students complete the task with an AI tool, saving the key prompts and outputs.

## Step 3 — Verification

Students check the AI's contributions against the textbook, the week's readings, or classmates' work.

## Step 4 — Comparison

Students compare the solo and collaborative results. What changed? Was it better? Who deserves credit?

## Step 5 — Reflection

After class, students write the weekly reflection (see below).

# Weekly Laboratories

## Lab 1 — Two Answers

**Task:** Students answer "Why do some employees perform better than others?" using only their own knowledge. They then answer it with an AI tool and compare the two answers.

**Observe:** What did the AI add? What did it miss? Did you change your mind? Why?

**Connects to:** Simon (1991); Bankins et al. (2023).

---

## Lab 2 — Calibrating Trust

**Task:** Students receive five workplace scenarios (e.g., which candidate to interview, how to respond to a customer complaint). For each, an AI provides a recommendation. The instructor has seeded two of the scenarios with flawed AI advice. Students decide whether to follow each recommendation and rate their confidence.

**Observe:** Which cues led you to trust or distrust the AI? Was your trust cognitive, emotional, or both? Did you catch the flawed advice?

**Connects to:** Mayer, Davis & Schoorman (1995); Vuori et al. (2025).

---

## Lab 3 — Building Knowledge Together

**Task:** Students build a concept map of learning and perception concepts, first from memory, then extended with AI. Every AI-added concept must be verified against the textbook or marked as unverified.

**Observe:** How much of the AI's contribution was correct? Did you understand everything you added? Is there anything you now "know" only because the AI said so?

**Connects to:** Nonaka (1994); Anthony (2021).

---

## Lab 4 — Redesigning a Job

**Task:** Students choose a familiar job (for example, a co-op role) and rate its skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, and feedback. They then imagine the job after a specific AI tool is introduced and rate it again, calculating the motivating potential score before and after.

**Observe:** Which job characteristics did AI increase? Which did it decrease? Would you be more or less motivated?

**Connects to:** Hackman & Oldham (1976); Schulz et al. (2025).

---

## Lab 5 — The Leader's AI Advisor

**Task:** Acting as a team leader, students draft developmental feedback for a struggling employee described in a short scenario — first alone, then with AI assistance.

**Observe:** Did AI make the feedback better or more generic? Would the employee feel the leader knows them? What does this suggest for leader–member exchange?

**Connects to:** Graen & Uhl-Bien (1995); Liu et al. (2026).

---

## Lab 6 — The Hidden Profile

**Task:** Teams of four receive a hiring decision in which each member holds some unique information. Half the teams decide through discussion alone; the other half first submit their information to an AI tool that summarizes it for the group. Teams compare their decisions with the best choice.

**Observe:** Did the AI help the team surface unshared information? Did anyone defer to the AI summary instead of their own information?

**Connects to:** Stasser & Titus (1985); Zercher et al. (2025).

---

## Lab 7 — Speaking Up to the Machine

**Task:** Teams receive an AI-generated recommendation for a workplace problem that contains a subtle error. Teams must reach a decision and document whether, when, and how anyone challenged the AI.

**Observe:** Who spoke up? Was it easier to challenge the AI or a teammate who supported it? What would make it safer to question AI in a real team?

**Connects to:** Edmondson (1999); Erengin et al. (2025).

---

## Lab 8 — Fairness Audit

**Task:** Students evaluate a fictional AI résumé-screening tool using a set of anonymized, fictional candidate profiles. They compare the tool's rankings across groups, discuss different definitions of fairness, and recommend how HR staff should use the tool.

**Observe:** Which definition of fairness did you apply? Could the tool be fair under one definition and unfair under another? Who should decide?

**Connects to:** Ely & Thomas (2001); van den Broek et al. (2025).

---

## Lab 9 — Drawing the Expertise Line

**Task:** Students role-play a professional services team (e.g., accounting) deciding which of ten tasks should be done by people, by AI, or by both, and who is accountable for each.

**Observe:** Which tasks did you protect for humans, and why? Were your reasons about quality, identity, or accountability?

**Connects to:** March (1991); Faulconbridge et al. (2023).

---

## Lab 10 — Theory Update Studio

**Task:** Students select their strongest OB Theory Update from the term and ask AI to critique it. They revise it, accepting or rejecting each critique with a reason.

**Observe:** Which critiques improved your thinking? Which did you reject? What does your final theory say about how humans and AI know and decide together?

**Connects to:** Murray, Rhymer & Sirmon (2021); Stelmaszak et al. (2025).

---

# Weekly Reflection

Each lab is followed by a written reflection (400–600 words), due before the next class week begins.

## Part A — Lab Log (about 200 words)

- What did the AI contribute?
- What did you contribute?
- What changed in your knowledge or your decision, and was the change an improvement?

## Part B — Reading Connection (about 150 words)

Connect your lab experience to the week's classic and contemporary readings.

## Part C — OB Theory Update (about 150 words)

> *"If [this week's classic theory] were rewritten for workplaces where people and AI build knowledge and make decisions together, I would change… because [evidence]."*

## Part D — AI Disclosure

A brief statement of how AI was used in writing the reflection (see Part IX).

The best 9 of 10 reflections count toward the final grade.

---

# AI Usage in the Labs

- Students may use any AI tool approved by the university.
- Students must save the prompts and outputs used in each lab.
- Students must never enter personal information about real people, including classmates, co-op colleagues, or members of the organization in their project.
- Scenarios, candidate profiles, and case data used in labs are fictional unless stated otherwise.

---

# Learning Outcomes

Through the labs, students will:

- experience how AI changes their own knowledge and decisions (CLO 3, CLO 5);
- practice verifying and questioning AI outputs (CLO 5);
- apply OB concepts to concrete tasks (CLO 2);
- and examine fairness and accountability in AI-supported decisions (CLO 4).
