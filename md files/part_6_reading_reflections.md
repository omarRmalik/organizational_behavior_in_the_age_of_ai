# Part VI
# Weekly Reading Reflections

> *"Reading a theory tells you what it says. Asking what it assumes tells you where it might break."*

---

# Introduction

Beginning in Week 2, each week pairs a classic organizational behavior article with a contemporary study of AI at work. The weekly reading reflection is where students make sense of that pairing.

Each reflection asks the same three things: What does the classic theory explain and assume? What does the AI study show? How should the theory change when humans and AI jointly develop knowledge and make decisions?

Over nine weeks, these short pieces of writing build the habit at the center of the course: examining familiar theories carefully enough to see where they hold, where they bend, and where they need new ideas.

---

# Learning Philosophy

The reflections are built on four ideas:

1. **Understand before critiquing.** Students first explain the classic theory accurately and fairly.
2. **Find the assumptions.** The most important question about any OB theory in this course is what it assumes about who knows and who decides.
3. **Use evidence.** Proposed changes must rest on what the AI study, the case, or other credible research actually shows.
4. **Be specific.** A good theory update names exactly what should change, not simply that "AI changes things."

# Reflection Structure

Each reflection is 400–600 words and has three parts.

## Part A — Reading Connection (about 250 words)

- What behavior does the classic theory explain, and what is its central claim?
- What does it assume about who holds knowledge and who makes decisions?
- What does the AI study find, and which assumption does it support, challenge, or change?

## Part B — OB Theory Update (about 200 words)

Respond to the prompt:

> *"If [this week's classic theory] were rewritten for workplaces where people and AI build knowledge and make decisions together, I would change… because [evidence]. This matters most for JKD Question __ at the __ level."*

## Part C — AI Disclosure

A brief statement of how AI was or was not used in preparing the reflection (see Part IX).

---

# What a Strong Theory Update Looks Like

**Weak:**

> *"Trust theory needs to change because people trust AI differently."*

**Strong:**

> *"Mayer, Davis, and Schoorman define trust through ability, benevolence, and integrity, assuming the trustee is a person with intentions. For AI, benevolence has no clear meaning, yet Vuori et al. (2025) show that employees' emotional trust in AI still shapes whether they use it. I would replace benevolence with 'perceived alignment' — the belief that the system was designed and governed in the user's interest — because this captures the emotional component without assuming the AI has intentions. This matters most for JKD Question 5 at the individual level."*

The strong version names the original assumption, cites evidence, proposes a specific change, and explains why the change fits the evidence.

---

# Using AI in Reflections

Students may use AI to help them understand difficult passages, check their summary of a theory, or test the strength of their theory update. Students may not use AI to write the reflection or to summarize readings they have not read. Every reflection includes an AI disclosure.

---

# Assessment

Each reflection is scored out of 10 using the rubric in Part VIII:

| Criterion | Points |
|---|---:|
| Reading Connection | 4 |
| OB Theory Update | 5 |
| AI Disclosure | 1 |

There are nine reflections. The best 8 of 9 count, for 20% of the course grade.

---

# From Reflections to the Work Study

The strongest theory updates should not stay in the reflections. In the final report of the Human–AI Work Study (Part VII), each team selects the classic OB theory that most needs revision in its organization and develops a theory update grounded in the organization's evidence. Students are encouraged to build on their own weekly reflections.

---

# Learning Outcomes

Through the reflections, students will:

- explain classic OB theories accurately (CLO 1);
- analyze how human–AI collaboration challenges their assumptions (CLO 3);
- and use AI responsibly and transparently in their own learning (CLO 5).
